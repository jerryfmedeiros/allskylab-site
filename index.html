All-Sky Lab

Measuring the night sky as an observing resource.

Calgary, Alberta

Most of what we know about the night sky comes from records built for other purposes: airport weather reports, satellite cloud products, volunteer photometer networks. Each carries its own biases, from the observer, from calibration, and from the sky itself changing. All-Sky Lab measures those biases and corrects for them, so that public and networked data can say how much usable sky a place actually has. Every paper ships with the code and data needed to rebuild its numbers.

Research interests

  1. Observer bias

    Human observers judge cloud by eye, and after dark they see less of it. How much clearer do staffed stations report the night than it really is?

  2. Calibration bias

    Instruments and products change underneath a record: a satellite cloud algorithm replaced mid-series, photometers whose zero points shift without notice. Finding and correcting those steps before reading trends from them.

  3. Natural bias

    The sky itself varies. Airglow brightens with the solar cycle, and left unmodelled that brightening reads as growth in light pollution.

  4. The observing resource

    Putting the records back together: how many nights a year are clear, dark, transparent, and free of smoke, city by city, once the cloud reports are corrected for their nighttime optimism.

  5. Methods for messy public data

    The statistics underneath all four. Choosing the unit of analysis so that correlated reports are not counted as independent evidence; comparison designs, such as difference-of-differences against independent references, that isolate one bias at a time; detecting step changes inside long instrument records; and uncertainties from resampling and alternative model choices rather than a single fit. Every number is regenerated from the released data and checked by script.

The all-sky station is an independent check on all of these: a sky record made at night without a human observer, with its own calibration documented from the start.

All-sky station

Live sky over Calgary

A low-cost station in Calgary pairs the visible fisheye camera with a thermal infrared sensor and a surface weather station. The two cameras are co-registered, so cloud can be mapped at night, when a visible camera alone sees little, and checked against GOES satellite scenes over the site.

The weather station (GW7426) reports to the Citizen Weather Observer Program, where its readings are quality-checked against the surrounding network. Live data · data-quality record

Current work is on cloud classification from the paired frames and a weatherproof dual-camera enclosure for unattended operation. The instrument and its record will be documented and released in the same way as the papers.

Papers

Submitted · Journal of Atmospheric and Oceanic Technology

Human cloud reports overstate nighttime clearing: a ceilometer, GOES, and VIIRS comparison at eight Canadian airports

Staffed stations at all eight of Canada's busiest airports report 7–14 points more clear sky at night than by day. A paired automated ceilometer and two independent satellite cloud masks find almost no real nighttime clearing: the excess comes from the observer, not the sky.

Submitted · Journal of the Royal Astronomical Society of Canada

Counting usable observing nights from public data: cloud, transparency, and smoke in eight Canadian cities, 2020–2025

How many nights a year have at least four hours of clear, dark sky, and how many of those are also transparent and free of wildfire smoke. Corrected for the bias above, the cities have 58–98 clear dark nights a year and 48–70 good ones; Winnipeg and Calgary have the most.

In preparation

The cycle-25 brightening of the natural night sky across the STARS4ALL TESS-W network and its effect on light-pollution trends

Measured on clear, moonless nights at 83 photometer sites from solar minimum to maximum, the natural sky brightens by about 0.14 mag as solar activity rises, less than most published values. Left unmodelled, that brightening reads as growth in light pollution.
